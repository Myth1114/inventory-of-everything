# Paper

## Overview

Paper is a thin, flexible sheet material made primarily from processed plant fibres. It is used for writing, printing, drawing, packaging, wrapping, and countless other purposes. Its ability to hold information in a lightweight, portable, and reproducible form made it one of the most influential materials in human history.

Before paper became widespread, societies recorded information on many different surfaces, including clay tablets, stone, wooden boards, bamboo strips, animal skins, and papyrus. Each material had its own advantages and limitations. Paper offered a comparatively lightweight and adaptable alternative that could be produced in sheets and assembled into documents and books.

Paper is closely connected to the development of education, administration, literature, religion, science, commerce, and printing. Its history is not simply the story of one invention, but of a manufacturing technique that developed in China and gradually spread across Asia, the Islamic world, and Europe.

Although the words are related, paper and papyrus are not the same material. Papyrus was made by arranging strips cut from the inner stem of the papyrus plant and pressing them into sheets. Paper is formed from separated fibres suspended in water and collected into a connected sheet.

## Origins and Early History

The earliest known papermaking traditions developed in ancient China. Archaeological discoveries have established that paper existed during the Western Han dynasty, centuries before the traditionally celebrated date of 105 CE.

Fragments of early paper have been discovered at several Chinese archaeological sites. Some date to the second century BCE and were made from processed plant fibres, including hemp and other bast fibres. Their quality and intended uses varied. Early paper was not necessarily designed exclusively for writing; some examples may have served as wrapping or other practical materials.

A major figure in the history of paper is Cai Lun, an official serving the Eastern Han imperial court. According to the historical account preserved in the _Book of the Later Han_, he presented an improved papermaking process to Emperor He in 105 CE.

Cai Lun is traditionally credited with improving and promoting papermaking through the use of materials such as tree bark, hemp waste, old cloth, and discarded fishing nets. His contribution helped establish paper as a more widely available and useful writing material. However, archaeological evidence shows that he did not invent paper from nothing.

Over time, paper production became increasingly established throughout China. It supported government administration, record keeping, correspondence, literature, education, and religious activity. As production techniques improved, different grades and varieties of paper were developed for different purposes.

## How It Works

Traditional papermaking transforms plant fibres into a continuous sheet. Although the tools and materials have changed considerably, the basic principle remains similar in handmade and industrial production.

Plant materials contain cellulose fibres, which provide the structure needed to form paper. Depending on the region and period, papermakers used materials such as hemp, mulberry bark, bamboo, flax, cotton rags, and other fibrous plants.

Traditional papermaking generally involves several stages:

- **Fibre preparation:** Suitable plant materials are collected, cleaned, and separated into usable fibres.
- **Soaking and cooking:** Fibres may be soaked or boiled to soften them and help remove unwanted substances.
- **Pulping:** The softened material is beaten, pounded, or mechanically processed until its fibres can be dispersed in water.
- **Sheet formation:** The pulp is suspended in water and collected on a porous screen or mould. Water drains away while the fibres form a thin, interconnected layer.
- **Pressing:** Excess water is removed, and the wet sheet becomes more compact.
- **Drying:** The sheet is dried on a surface or suspended in air.
- **Finishing:** Depending on its intended use, the paper may be smoothed, sized, coated, or otherwise treated.

The arrangement and bonding of the fibres give paper its strength and flexibility. The length and condition of the fibres, the amount of processing, and the manufacturing method influence the final sheet's properties.

Modern papermaking uses similar principles but employs industrial equipment. Pulp is prepared and refined, distributed across a moving wire mesh, drained, pressed, and dried through a continuous production process.

Different papers require different characteristics. Writing paper, newsprint, packaging board, tissue, and specialty papers are manufactured with different fibre mixtures, thicknesses, surface treatments, and performance requirements.

## How It Evolved

Papermaking developed over centuries as craftsmen experimented with different fibres, tools, and production methods.

### Expansion across East Asia

From China, papermaking techniques spread to other parts of East Asia. Paper production became established in Korea and Japan, where local craftsmen adapted the process to available materials and their own cultural requirements.

In Japan, traditional handmade paper became known as _washi_. It was produced using long plant fibres, particularly from paper mulberry, and became valued for its strength, texture, and versatility.

Paper also became important for religious texts, painting, calligraphy, official records, and decorative arts. These developments demonstrate how a manufacturing technique could evolve differently across regions while retaining its fundamental principles.

### Central Asia and the Islamic world

Papermaking and the use of paper gradually expanded westward into Central Asia. Samarkand became an important centre associated with early paper production outside China.

The popular account that Chinese papermakers were captured during the Battle of Talas in 751 CE and immediately introduced the technique to the Islamic world is not firmly established. The precise route and circumstances of transmission remain debated.

What is clearer is that paper became increasingly important across the Islamic lands during the early medieval period. By the late eighth century, paper production was established in Baghdad. It subsequently spread through regions including Iran, Syria, Egypt, and North Africa.

Paper supported an expanding culture of written scholarship, administration, commerce, and literature. It became an important material for manuscripts, scientific writing, religious texts, correspondence, and record keeping.

### Introduction into Europe

Papermaking reached parts of Europe through contact with the Islamic world, particularly through Mediterranean connections involving Spain and Italy.

European paper production developed gradually. Early mills relied heavily on linen and cotton rags, which were collected, cleaned, processed, and converted into pulp.

By the thirteenth century, paper mills were operating in parts of Italy. European papermakers developed their own production techniques and equipment, including improvements to moulds and the use of water-powered machinery to process rags.

Watermarks also became an important feature of European paper. These designs, formed during sheet production, helped identify the paper mill or manufacturer and eventually became useful for studying the history and authenticity of documents.

### Paper and printing

The development of paper and printing reinforced each other.

In China, paper was already established when woodblock printing developed. Printed Buddhist texts and other documents demonstrated the usefulness of producing multiple copies of written material.

Later, the development of movable-type printing in East Asia and Europe increased the importance of paper as a medium for reproducing information.

In Europe, Johannes Gutenberg's printing system in the fifteenth century helped expand the production and circulation of printed books. Paper was essential to this transformation because it provided a practical surface for printing at a scale that parchment could not easily support.

### Industrial papermaking

For centuries, most paper was produced by hand, one sheet at a time. This limited production speed and made the supply of suitable rags an important constraint.

A major change came in 1799, when French inventor Louis-Nicolas Robert developed a design for a continuous papermaking machine. The technology was subsequently developed further in Britain by Henry and Sealy Fourdrinier and their associates.

The Fourdrinier machine made it possible to produce a continuous web of paper rather than individual sheets. This became a foundation of modern industrial papermaking.

During the nineteenth century, manufacturers increasingly turned to wood as a source of fibre. Mechanical and chemical pulping processes made it possible to produce much larger quantities of paper and helped meet growing demand from newspapers, publishing, education, and packaging.

Modern paper production now uses a wide range of fibres and processing technologies, including recycled paper, wood pulp, agricultural residues, and specialty materials.

## Impact on the World

Paper changed how societies stored, communicated, and distributed information.

Before its widespread adoption, many written records depended on materials that were expensive, heavy, difficult to prepare, or inconvenient to transport. Paper made written communication more practical and helped increase the volume of documents that could be produced and preserved.

Its impact can be seen across several areas.

- **Government and administration:** Paper supported taxation records, official correspondence, legal documents, maps, and administrative systems.
- **Education and scholarship:** Books, notebooks, manuscripts, and educational materials became easier to produce and circulate.
- **Science and mathematics:** Researchers used paper to record observations, develop calculations, exchange ideas, and preserve technical knowledge.
- **Religion and culture:** Religious texts, poetry, literature, calligraphy, painting, and historical records spread through paper-based manuscripts and printed works.
- **Commerce:** Paper enabled contracts, receipts, account books, invoices, financial records, and other business documents.
- **Printing and publishing:** Paper became the essential material supporting the large-scale production of newspapers, books, magazines, and other printed media.

Paper also influenced artistic traditions. Its different textures, weights, and surfaces made it suitable for drawing, painting, printmaking, calligraphy, and decorative crafts.

Its importance continued to grow as literacy, publishing, education, and industrial production expanded. Even after the development of digital communication, paper remained widely used in everyday life.

## Global History and Perspectives

Paper has a history shaped by multiple regions and cultural traditions.

China was the centre of the earliest established papermaking traditions. Its craftsmen developed techniques that transformed processed plant fibres into usable sheets and helped establish paper as an important material for administration, literature, and learning.

Korea and Japan adapted these methods and developed distinctive traditions of handmade paper. Japanese _washi_, for example, became valued for its durability and use in art, writing, and traditional crafts.

Central Asia played an important role in the movement of papermaking knowledge and materials toward western regions. The Islamic world expanded paper production and made paper central to many areas of scholarship, commerce, and administration.

Europe adopted and adapted papermaking through Mediterranean connections. Its mills, manufacturing improvements, and eventual industrial machinery transformed paper into a mass-produced material.

Paper production also developed in South Asia and other regions, where local fibres, crafts, and manufacturing traditions shaped the materials available to communities.

These histories show that paper was not simply transferred unchanged from one civilization to another. Each region adapted the material and its production to local resources, needs, and cultural practices.

## Challenges and the Future

Paper remains important, but its production creates environmental and resource challenges.

Traditional paper production depended heavily on plant fibres, water, and energy. Modern industrial manufacturing operates at a much larger scale and can place pressure on forests, water resources, and energy systems.

Wood-pulp production, chemical processing, bleaching, drying, and transportation all have environmental impacts. The overall footprint depends on the raw materials, energy sources, manufacturing methods, and waste-management systems involved.

Several approaches are being used to improve the sustainability of paper production:

- **Recycled fibres:** Recovering fibres from used paper can reduce the demand for newly sourced raw materials, although fibres become shorter and weaker after repeated recycling.
- **Responsible forestry:** Sustainable forest management and certified sourcing can help reduce pressure on natural ecosystems.
- **Alternative fibres:** Agricultural residues and other plant materials can provide additional sources of cellulose where technically and economically suitable.
- **Cleaner manufacturing:** Improved water treatment, energy efficiency, and chemical recovery can reduce the environmental impact of production.
- **Better product design:** Reducing unnecessary packaging and choosing suitable paper grades can limit material consumption.

Digital communication has also changed the role of paper. Electronic documents, online publishing, and digital storage have replaced some traditional uses, but paper remains important in packaging, hygiene products, education, printing, and many other applications.

The future of paper will depend on balancing its practical value with responsible sourcing, efficient manufacturing, recycling, and the development of improved materials.

## Related Inventions and Ideas

- **Printing Press:** A technology that dramatically increased the ability to reproduce written information on paper.
- **Papyrus:** An ancient writing material made from the papyrus plant, distinct from true paper.
- **Ink:** A material used to create written and printed marks on paper.
- **Books and Manuscripts:** Formats that use paper to preserve and organize written knowledge.
- **Woodblock Printing:** An early printing method that helped expand the use of paper in East Asia.
- **Movable Type:** A printing technology that made it possible to rearrange individual characters and reproduce texts.
- **Printing Press:** A major development in the mass production and circulation of printed material.
- **Recycling:** A process that recovers usable fibres from discarded paper products.
